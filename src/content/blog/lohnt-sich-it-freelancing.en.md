When employees hear a freelancer's hourly rate, they usually do the math in their head: 95 euros times 40 hours times 52 weeks comes to almost 200,000 euros a year. Reality looks different...

I've been a freelancer since 2009 and have done this calculation myself often enough. Here it is, honestly, with rounded example figures. After that come the items that the usual calculators leave out.

## What's left of the hourly rate

As an example I'll use a rate of 95 euros. That's roughly the median freelancermap reports for IT freelancers in 2026.

Out of 250 working days, take away 30 days of vacation, 10 days of sickness and 10 days for acquisition, bookkeeping and admin. That leaves 200 days, or 1,600 hours. Then there's the gap between projects. Assuming three months without a contract every three years, another 130 hours or so go missing each year. What's left is just under 1,500 billable hours and around 140,000 euros in revenue.

Out of that, the freelancer pays everything that an employer covers half of for an employee:

| Item | per year |
|---|---:|
| Revenue | €140,000 |
| Health and long-term care insurance | −€15,000 |
| Retirement savings at statutory pension level (both halves) | −€18,000 |
| Unemployment insurance, disability insurance, liability insurance | −€4,000 |
| Hardware, software, tax advisor, courses | −€6,000 |
| **Left before income tax** | **€97,000** |

For comparison, take an employed senior developer earning 80,000 euros gross a year. That's within the usual range: the Federal Employment Agency's Entgeltatlas lists a median of around 6,300 euros gross per month for full-time software developers. After the employee's share of social security contributions, around 64,000 euros remain before tax, and they're covered for health, pension and unemployment.

On paper, then, the freelancer is a good 30,000 euros ahead. That's the point where most calculations stop.

## The items no calculator includes

First, default risk. In 2024 an agency I was working through filed for insolvency. My work from June to mid-September was never paid, three and a half months in total. The project was gone the same day, even though the end client was happy. After that it took me another three and a half months to find a new project at short notice. Altogether that was seven months without income.

The three and a half months of searching are already covered by the gap the calculation above works with. The three and a half months of work that were never paid are not. In the example calculation that's around 40,000 euros. Employees in that situation receive insolvency pay for the last three months. Freelancers file their claim and wait for a payout ratio. If something like this happens once in 15 years, it costs just under 3,000 euros a year when spread out. On top of that comes the rate you accept out of necessity when you get back in.

Second, severance. It isn't guaranteed: anyone who quits gets none, and not everyone stays at the same company for 15 years. But anyone laid off for operational reasons after many years with a company usually gets one. The statutory guideline under Section 1a of the German Dismissal Protection Act is half a month's salary per year of service. After 15 years at 80,000 euros, that's 50,000 euros, or a good 3,000 euros a year spread out. Add to that notice periods of several months and unemployment benefits afterwards. A freelancer gets their last invoice paid at the end of a project, if all goes well.

Third, long-term illness. The calculation includes ten sick days a year. An employee who is ill for longer gets six weeks of full pay, then sickness benefit of around 70 percent of gross salary, capped at the contribution ceiling. A freelancer has no income at all for the first weeks, and after that only if they've opted into sickness benefit or taken out daily sickness insurance. Being out for three months costs them a quarter of their annual revenue.

Fourth, training in your own time. Freelancers who learn a new stack usually do it in the evenings and at weekends. At 150 hours a year, that's almost four working weeks nobody pays for. It's hard to put a euro figure on it, because you couldn't have billed those hours anyway. Employees more often get that time during working hours. Why freelancers can't avoid these hours is explained in the section on technology change further down.

Fifth, parental support. If your parents move into a care home and their money doesn't cover it, social welfare pays and can then reclaim the money from the children. Since 2020 there's been a threshold of 100,000 euros annual income for this. For employees, gross salary counts; for freelancers it's profit, before health insurance and retirement savings. The employee on 80,000 euros is below it. The freelancer from the example is well above it with around 134,000 euros in profit, even though only 97,000 euros remain after insurance and retirement savings. So the threshold hits them earlier than their actual income would suggest. On top of that, the federal government wants to lower the threshold or scrap it entirely. Nothing has been decided yet.

Taking off default risk and severance leaves a lead of around 27,000 euros before tax. Income tax still has to come off that, at a marginal rate of around 42 percent at these income levels. That leaves just under 16,000 euros net a year, around 1,300 euros a month. In return, the freelancer works four weeks a year unpaid and carries the risk of a long illness alone.

## Starting over every few years

The gap between projects in the calculation above isn't pessimism. In Germany it's part of the model.

The reason is called bogus self-employment (Scheinselbstständigkeit). Hardly any company knows exactly when a freelancer counts as falsely self-employed, and since the Federal Social Court's Herrenberg ruling in 2022, the uncertainty has, if anything, grown. The result is rarely a clear no. More often it's an unspoken rule: after two or three years the collaboration ends. Not because the client is unhappy, but because nobody wants to carry the risk in case the pension insurance authority takes a closer look.

It hit me back in 2016. After two years at an aerospace company it was over, because the boss of the service provider I was placed through had watched a TV documentary about bogus self-employment. It had nothing to do with my work. Since then I've seen the same thing happen to many colleagues: terminations nobody wanted, out of fear of a problem nobody could describe precisely.

For the freelancer, that means regularly looking for a new project, getting up to speed on a new system and negotiating again, often from a weaker position than the one they just left.

## The network carries less than you think

The usual answer is: that's what your network is for. In practice it helps less often than it should.

Teams you worked in no longer exist after a few years. People have moved on, the department was restructured. Companies where you were welcome no longer hire freelancers, because of bogus self-employment or to cut costs. And the longer ago a project was, the fewer people are still there whom you could call. After ten years, a client contact is usually little more than a name on LinkedIn.

Then there's timing. Inquiries almost always come when you're fully booked. When you're looking yourself, it's quiet. So the network mostly helps when you don't need it.

## What 20 years of technology change cost

When I started, Java EE with JSF was the standard in every large company. A few years later everyone wanted Spring Boot on the backend and Angular on the frontend. Today clients ask how to bring AI agents into development. Anyone who was a JSF expert in 2012 will hardly find projects for it today.

Every one of these shifts costs the freelancer the same thing: they learn the new stack in their own time, build up references and then have to find the first client willing to buy the new technology from them, even though they haven't used it in three projects yet. An employee usually moves along when the company migrates, and learns at the company's expense.

The bitter part is that there's no higher rate at the end of all that work. You've only kept it from dropping.

## The "developer" role is stagnating

The numbers show it. According to freelancermap's Freelancer-Kompass, the average hourly rate in development was 74 euros in 2016 and 94 euros in 2025. That's around 27 percent more. Consumer prices rose by about 28 percent over the same period. In real terms, then, a freelance developer earned as much in 2025 as in 2016. In 2026, the average rate across all fields failed to rise for the first time since the survey began.

My own numbers look the same. My rate has risen by a good 40 percent in nominal terms since 2009, and so have prices. In good years I earned more by working more hours, not through a better rate.

Things look little better for employees. According to the Federal Statistical Office, full-time employees in the IT services sector earned an average of around 4,460 euros gross per month in 2010 and around 5,560 euros in 2021, excluding special payments and across all jobs in the sector. That's 25 percent more against 17 percent inflation, so about 6 percent in real terms over eleven years, or a good half percent a year. Newer figures can't be compared directly, because the survey was changed in 2022.

So for both, the "developer" role pays hardly better than ten years ago. The difference lies in who pays for the way out. The market pays freelancers for the role, not for years of experience. "Angular developer, full-time, six months" has a market price, and it doesn't change just because you've been doing it for 15 years. An employee can become a lead or an architect within their company. People know them, give them the new responsibility and pay them while they grow into it. Freelancers get hired for what they've demonstrably done already.

In the Freelancer-Kompass, consulting and management averaged 120 euros in 2025. The way there leads through a different role: architecture, consulting, training, leadership. Except nobody promotes you as a freelancer. You have to work your way into it on the side, make it visible and then find someone who books you in the new role first. Again in your own time, again at your own risk.

## So is it worth it?

Purely by the numbers: yes, but much less than the hourly rate suggests. After all items, around 1,300 euros net a month more is left than for an employee, at least as long as utilization holds, you stay healthy and no agency goes under. For that extra, though, you regularly learn a new stack in your own time, look for a new project every few years and give up dismissal protection, severance and insolvency pay.

It only becomes truly worthwhile once you get out of the "developer" role. That's possible, but it's a second job on top of the first. Freelancers who just want to be good developers earn the same in real terms as ten years ago and work more unpaid hours for it than an employee.

Then there's AI, which is currently taking over exactly the implementation work freelancers used to be hired for. I wrote about that in my [last article](/blog/was-bleibt-fuer-it-freelancer).
