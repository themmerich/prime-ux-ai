I've been an IT freelancer for 20 years, these days as a frontend architect and coach. I've seen a few upheavals in that time. This one feels different.

Most articles about AI and freelancing are reassuring: AI only replaces routine work, experience will become more valuable, those who adapt will win in the end. I want to try something different here, argument by argument, without sedatives and without exempting myself.

## What we were actually hired for

Let's be honest: most freelancers were never hired because a company needed our judgment. They were hired because the team couldn't get through the backlog. "We need two Angular developers for six months" was the typical engagement for years.

The second reason was making teams better: bringing knowledge in-house, coaching developers, establishing standards. AI can now take over both, at least in part. It writes the code and explains it to the team at the same time, around the clock and without a notice period.

The AI doesn't have to be better than a freelancer. It just has to be good enough that procurement stops posting the position.

Then there's the current climate. Most companies are waiting it out: they keep their people, but they neither hire nor lay off. For permanent employees, that's a phase of uncertainty. For freelancers, it's already the crisis.

Because externals are the buffer. Whoever waits doesn't extend contracts and doesn't book new ones. That's why freelancers feel the shift first, before it's even clear how it will play out.

## The safe havens are too small

The usual reassurance goes: AI writes the code, but architecture, reviews, requirements analysis and consulting stay with humans. That may be true. The problem isn't whether these activities survive, but how many people they need.

A project that used to employ eight developers may still need one person who makes architecture decisions. And that person usually already works at the company.

Even these islands are being nibbled at. AI makes architecture proposals, finds bugs in reviews and turns meeting notes into user stories. The safe havens are real, but there isn't room there for everyone currently being pushed out of implementation work.

## Senior or junior: the freelancer loses either way

Nobody knows right now where this is heading. Will companies need experienced developers who do good work with AI? Or will juniors be enough to achieve the same? Looked at pessimistically, the answer doesn't matter for freelancers, because both scenarios end badly.

Scenario A, seniors with AI: an experienced developer with AI replaces a small team. Then a company only needs a few seniors, and it usually already has them in-house. They know the system, the people and the politics, so why bring in an expensive external?

Scenario B, juniors with AI: if a junior with AI delivers what a senior used to deliver, experience is devalued. But experience is exactly what senior freelancers sell, at corresponding hourly rates. Then the external is redundant and, on top of that, the most expensive item on the list.

Freelancers are betting that experience will become more valuable. But even if that's true, the first to benefit are the experienced people already sitting inside the company. I'm among those hoping for scenario A myself, and I don't know whether the math works out.

## More productive, but not richer

Clients read the same headlines we do: with AI, developers are twice as fast. They draw a simple conclusion from that: then it should also cost half as much.

This hits the hourly model first. Whoever bills by the hour and gets faster with AI earns less, because the productivity gain lands with the client. Whoever works slowly to keep the hours up is no longer competitive.

The escape route of "fixed prices, then" sounds good but doesn't help much. The client knows the shortcut too by now, estimates the effort with AI's help if necessary, and considers every quote overpriced.

Competition does the rest. Because there's less work, more freelancers compete for fewer engagements, and whoever comes away empty-handed lowers their price. Hourly rates come under pressure from two sides: from clients and from colleagues.

Rates are already falling. Whether that's due to AI or the general market situation is hard to untangle. But that's exactly the problem.

In the past, a slump was followed by an upswing, and with it engagements and rates came back. This time, companies are learning during the slump to get by with fewer people. The dip could become the new normal.

## The context advantage is melting away

The strongest argument for humans usually goes: AI doesn't know the system. It doesn't know the project's history, the unwritten rules and the pitfalls in the legacy code.

This argument is losing force. AI agents are getting access to the repository, tickets, wiki and chat histories. They're not yet perfect at grasping large codebases, but they're catching up.

For freelancers there's an uncomfortable addition: they, of all people, have the least context. They come from outside, stay a few months and leave again.

Onboarding a freelancer has always been a cost that clients accepted. Onboarding an AI costs almost nothing. The argument "AI lacks context" therefore mainly protects the permanent employees who have been embedded in the system for years.

## Responsibility stays with humans, but not with the freelancer

In the end, a human has to answer for things: for security holes, outages and wrong decisions. That's true, but who is this human? In almost all companies, it's someone internal, the team lead, the product owner or the CTO.

They approve the pull request and carry the responsibility towards management. Whether the code came from a freelancer or from an AI beforehand changes little about that.

In Germany there's something else. Most freelancers work under a services contract, not a contract for work: they owe their working time, not a result. And because of the risk of false self-employment, many clients deliberately avoid embedding externals too deeply in decision-making structures.

So responsibility was never our selling point. It doesn't work as a lifeline now either. Especially since companies are pragmatic about risk: if the AI solution is almost as good and significantly cheaper, many accept the residual risk.

## Who comes next?

When companies stop hiring, juniors are hit first. Entry-level tasks like small bugfixes, simple components or tests are taken over by AI. That removes the path on which developers used to gather experience.

It was exactly these people who later became seniors and often freelancers too. So the profession is drying out from below: hardly anyone new comes up, networks thin out, agencies withdraw. At the same time, the industry is losing the knowledge of how systems actually work.

You can read this differently, too. If hardly any new talent comes up, the remaining seniors will be scarce in ten years, and therefore valuable. Maybe. It's a bet whose outcome nobody knows, and whether you'll still be in the market to cash it in is a second one.

## What realistically remains

Something remains, but for far fewer people. I see four islands.

The most obvious one is firefighting: when a system is on fire, a project is tipping over, or an AI-generated mountain of code is no longer maintainable, you bring in someone to clean up. Well paid, but short, irregular and hard to plan.

Then deep specialization. Whoever truly belongs to the best in a narrow niche will keep getting booked. By definition, that's few people.

Regulated environments like banking, medicine, government and critical infrastructure slow down AI adoption through compliance and liability questions. That's more of a postponement than lasting protection.

And finally trust and network: clients who have worked with a particular person for years stay with them. But winning new clients this way is getting harder.

For the broad mass who have delivered solid implementation work until now, little remains: fewer engagements, lower rates, longer gaps. Some go back to permanent employment, if there are positions, others leave the industry. What remains is not a profession but a handful of niches, and for each of them there are more applicants than places.

## No all-clear

Whoever claims that not much will change for IT freelancers is selling sedatives. Whoever wants to stay anyway has only a few levers: position yourself on one of the islands, specialize so deeply that you're among the first choices there, and tend your network while it still carries. There's no guarantee in any of it.

I'm going to try exactly that. Not because I'm sure it will be enough, but because I don't see a better option. If you're a freelancer yourself: I'm curious how you see it.
